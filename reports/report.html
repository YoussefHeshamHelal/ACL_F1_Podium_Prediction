1. Task, unit of analysis and prediction cutoff

Task. For every driver entering a race, predict whether the driver finishes on the podium (positionOrder ≤ 3). This is binary classification. The unit of analysis is one driver in one race, so (raceId, driverId) is unique in the modeling table.

Prediction cutoff: after qualifying, before the start. The grid is fixed and the lights are not yet out.

InformationStatus at the cutoff
Allowed (known before the start)grid slot and pit-lane start; qualifying position; championship standings after the previous race; form, reliability, ratings and history from previous races; circuit and calendar facts
Leakage (never a feature)this race's position / positionText / positionOrder, points, laps, time, status, fastest lap, pit stops, lap times, and standings after this race

How leakage is prevented in code.

  • Races are ordered by date, and every history feature is shifted by one race, so the current race is never included.
  • Standings are taken from the previous race in the calendar. A season opener uses the previous season's final table and is flagged.
  • Time-travel test (§4.5): we recomputed the features for four races after deleting all later races and blanking each race's own result. 0 of 3,510 values changed.

2. Data audit and cleaning

2.1 Audit of the 14 raw tables (§1)

  • Keys: every primary key is unique and non-null, and all 23 foreign keys reconcile (no orphans).
  • The required grain is violated by 85 driver-race pairs (176 rows), all from shared drives in the 1950s and a few later seasons.
  • Missing values: the \N sentinel appears in 6 tables, and qualifying.q2/q3 also contains empty strings. Dates and lap or qualifying times are stored as text in several formats.
  • Qualifying-coverage drift (Figure 1): qualifying coverage is 0 % of entries before 1994, 88–100 % in 1994–95, only 6–59 % in 1996–2002, and about 100 % from 2003. For most of history the grid is the only record of the starting order.
  • grid = 0 on 1,638 rows, never a podium: mostly entries that did not (pre-)qualify, plus a few genuine pit-lane starts.
  • The base rate tracks the field size, at about 3 / entrants: 7.7–8.9 % in 1989–91, 15 % in 2022–24.
  • The Indianapolis 500 (1950–60, 11 races) is 18 % of the 1950–60 rows, and 103 of its 107 drivers never raced elsewhere.

3. Data-engineering questions

Every answer uses multi-hop joins, and every rate carries a Wilson 95 % confidence interval.

Q1. Which circuits convert a front-row start into a podium most often, and does that change after 2014?

Joins: results ⋈ races ⋈ circuits ⋈ qualifying. Eras: 2003–2013 vs the hybrid era 2014–2024 (qualifying is complete from 2003). A circuit needs at least 10 front-row starts per era.

Answer. Overall, a front-row start (grid P1–P2) converts 68 % → 76 % from 2003–13 to 2014–24 (by qualifying position: 67 % → 74 %).

  • Top circuits after 2014: Suzuka 89 %, Abu Dhabi 86 %, Monaco 85 %. The lowest is Budapest (59 %).
  • The ranking reshuffled: Spearman ρ = 0.21 (p = 0.47) over the 14 circuits with enough data in both eras. Abu Dhabi went from last to second; Monaco is high in both eras.

Qualifying position vs grid position. The circuit rankings by the two definitions are almost identical (ρ = 0.91 / 0.84). Where they differ, the grid matters:

  • front-row qualifiers who started further back after a penalty converted only 22 % / 31 %;
  • drivers promoted onto the front row converted 56 % / 67 %;
  • such cases tripled after 2014 (9 → 29), mostly engine-part penalties.

Grain / missingness / cutoff. One front-row starter per race (2 per race, 428 races). Qualifying is missing for 0.3 % of these rows. Grid and qualifying are both known before the start.

Caveat: per-circuit intervals are ±15–25 points, so the robust findings are the overall rise and the reshuffle.

Q2. Do drivers finish on the podium more often at home, controlling for starting position?

Joins: results ⋈ drivers (nationality → country) and results ⋈ races ⋈ circuits (circuit country). Comparison within grid bands P1, P2–3, P4–6, P7–10, P11+.

Answer: no evidence of a home advantage.

  • Raw comparison (confounded): home 11.9 % vs away 13.8 %. Home drivers start further back on average (grid 12.7 vs 11.7).
  • Within the same grid band: Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio 0.95 [0.80–1.13], 10,000-permutation p = 0.56.
  • Training seasons only (2003–2019): OR 0.71 [0.45–1.12], p = 0.13.

Grain / missingness / cutoff. One starter with a grid slot per race. Driver and circuit country are complete after the mapping (0 missing). "Home" and grid are both known before the start.

Limitation: with only 20 home pole starts in 2003–19, a small effect cannot be ruled out.

Q3. Which constructors have the highest mechanical-retirement rate, 2014–2021 vs 2022–2024?

Joins: results ⋈ status ⋈ constructors (team lineage) ⋈ races. Mechanical = a car component failed; crashes, driver errors and regulation decisions are excluded. A team needs at least 40 starts per era.

Answer. Mechanical failures ended 9.9 % of starts in 2014–21 and 6.1 % in 2022–24 (χ² p ≈ 5·10⁻⁵).

  • 2014–21: highest were Alpine lineage (Lotus F1 / Renault) 15.0 %, RB lineage (Toro Rosso / AlphaTauri) 14.7 %, McLaren 14.4 %. Mercedes was lowest at 4.1 %.
  • 2022–24: highest were Alpine 11.0 %, Sauber 8.8 %, RB and Ferrari 6.6 %. McLaren and Red Bull were lowest at 3.7 %.
  • Alpine is the least reliable team in both eras; McLaren, RB and Red Bull improved most.

Robustness: a strict definition (without tyre / wheel / wing statuses) gives the same ranking (ρ = 0.97 / 1.00).

Grain / missingness / cutoff. One race start per row (4,623 starts), status complete. Status is post-race information, used here descriptively only. Past reliability is a feature; the current race's status never is.

Encoding. All 38 features are numeric (positions, counts, rates, ranks, ratings, 0/1 flags), so no label or one-hot encoding is needed. Categorical sources (driver, team, circuit, nationality, status) become the pre-race summaries above. We avoided identity columns for three reasons:

  • they would let the models memorise names instead of learning signals that carry over to new seasons;
  • teams that are new in the test seasons would have no column;
  • they would add about 1,000 sparse columns.

Evidence (training seasons only).

  • Strongest single features: qualifying position, grid, circuit grid prior, form, standings and Elo (ROC-AUC 0.89–0.91).
  • No single feature exceeds 0.91, so there is no leakage signal.
  • Weak features (ROC-AUC 0.50–0.59) are kept as modifiers.

Tested and rejected:

  • past pit-stop performance: PR-AUC −0.024;
  • qualifying lap-time gaps on top of position: −0.0002 / −0.0037, CIs include 0;
  • sprint results: no sprint weekends in training, and already reflected in the grid (2021–22) and the standings;
  • near-duplicates: ρ = 0.99;
  • home_race: no effect in Q2 and no gain in the ablation.

  • Why split by season? Deployment predicts future races from past ones. Rows of one race share conditions, so a random split would mix the same race across splits. A random-row split showed no inflation (0.620 vs 0.634), consistent with leakage-safe features.
  • Training window: all seasons are kept, with recency weights 0.5^((2019 − season)/5). They beat 2003–19-only training (validation PR-AUC ≈ 0.72 vs 0.70).
  • Qualifying for the early seasons: qualifying position is derived from the grid where no qualifying row exists. This is exact for 98.2 % of 1994–2002 drivers, and every derived value is flagged.

Preprocessing-order experiments (validation PR-AUC, Figure 10):

  • (A) Fitting the imputer and scaler on all rows vs training rows only changed PR-AUC by −0.001 (CI includes 0). But the evaluation seasons moved the training means by up to 15 %, so the preprocessing is fitted on training rows only, inside each pipeline.
  • (B) A circuit grid prior computed from all seasons, including the podiums being predicted, gave +0.035 for logistic regression (CI [+0.006, +0.060]). This is target leakage that inflates the score, so the prior uses past races only.
  • (C) Missing-value handling: median + flags for LR / NN; native NaN for the trees (0.726 vs 0.710 for imputed boosting).

Missing values. They are "no history yet" (debuts, first visits, the seasons before the constructors' championship existed). The rows are kept: missingness itself is informative (debut podium rate 2.5 % vs 13.8 %), and dropping the incomplete training rows changed PR-AUC by +0.0007 (CI [−0.018, +0.021]).

6. Models and evaluation

6.1 Models (same table, same 38 features, same rows)

  • Logistic regression (baseline): a weighted sum of the scaled features through a sigmoid, with an L2 penalty. Simple and stable; additive in the log-odds.
  • Random forest: 300 decision trees on bootstrap samples, with random feature subsets at each split; their votes are averaged. Captures thresholds and interactions; handles NaN natively.
  • Gradient boosting (HistGradientBoostingClassifier): small trees added one after another, each correcting the remaining error. Usually the strongest tree model on tables.
  • Shallow neural network (Keras): 1–2 hidden ReLU layers and a sigmoid output, trained with Adam on binary cross-entropy, with early stopping on validation PR-AUC.

6.2 Metrics

  • Primary: PR-AUC. Podiums are rare (15 %), and PR-AUC measures how well the podium finishers are ranked at the top. No skill scores 0.15.
  • Why not ROC-AUC? It rewards ranking the easy back-of-grid cases: the one-feature grid rule already reaches 0.91.
  • Secondary: F1 at the threshold that maximises F1 on validation, because a podium call is a yes/no decision.
  • Also reported: ROC-AUC, precision, recall, accuracy and the top-3 hit rate (the share of each race's three highest-scored drivers who actually finished on the podium).
  • Accuracy is not used for decisions. "Always no podium" is already 85 % accurate.

6.5 Limitations of each model

  • LR: additive in the log-odds; the correlated features (standings, form, Elo) need strong shrinkage, so individual coefficients are unstable; it extrapolates linearly beyond the training range.
  • RF: memorises unless the trees are kept shallow; cannot extrapolate beyond training values (season, team_starts); its built-in importances are biased, which is why we use permutation importance and SHAP.
  • GB: sensitive to the number of trees, chosen on only 39 races from 224 configurations; same extrapolation limit as the RF.
  • NN: least stable across settings and seeds; uses validation twice (early stopping and configuration), with the largest validation → test drop; miscalibrated by class weights; a black box.

7. Feature-group ablation and explainability

7.1 Feature-group ablation (§7.1)

Each variant is refitted with the frozen settings, without one group (or with only one group).

  • No single group is essential: every change in RF PR-AUC is under ±0.012, and almost every CI includes 0. The groups overlap; for example, starting position is also encoded in circuit_grid_prior.
  • Removing whole blocks costs more: grid + qualifying together −0.016 on validation; all past-performance groups −0.013.
  • Race context alone (8 features) matches all 38: test 0.715 vs 0.714.
  • Circuit history hurts on test: +0.011 [+0.001, +0.022] without it. It does not carry over into the 2022 rules era. The model was not changed, because the test set must not drive feature selection.
  • home_race adds nothing (−0.001 / −0.003).

7.2 Global explanations: permutation importance and SHAP (§7.2–7.3)

  • Permutation importance on the test seasons: the RF relies on circuit_grid_prior (−0.027 PR-AUC when shuffled), grid_f (−0.021) and quali_pos_d (−0.013). Every other feature costs under 0.003, because a substitute carries its information.
  • Grouped permutation: race context 0.067 > standings 0.027 > grid 0.019 > qualifying 0.009. Shuffling a whole group is needed because correlated features split their importance.
  • SHAP (exact TreeExplainer; additivity error about 1e-15, also on rows with NaN): share of the total effect — race context 29 %, standings 19 %, grid 13 %, driver form 13 %, qualifying 12 %, constructor form 8 %, ratings 4 %.
  • The grid effect is a step: P1–P3 add +0.08 to +0.11 to the probability, P4 about +0.035, P5 and further back −0.02 to −0.05.
  • The out-of-range features (season, team_starts) contribute under 0.01.
  • Logistic-regression coefficients (the intrinsically interpretable model): circuit_grid_prior +0.26, strong_cars_ahead −0.20, grid −0.16, qualifying −0.16 log-odds per SD. Every sign makes racing sense, and the ranking agrees with RF SHAP (Spearman ρ = 0.88).

7.3 Local explanation: one driver in one race (§7.4)

Row chosen by a fixed rule: the highest-probability test driver who started P4 or lower and did finish on the podium. That is Max Verstappen, 2024 British GP, who started P4 and finished P2. The model gave him p = 0.544, a podium call; the grid rule would have missed him.

  • SHAP waterfall, from the average 0.141: recent podium form +0.07, the historical podium rate of P4 at Silverstone +0.04, team leading the constructors' championship +0.03, himself leading the drivers' championship +0.03.
  • LIME (local fit R² 0.77) agrees on 9 of the top 10 features, all with the same direction.

Importance is not causation. These methods explain the model, not Formula 1. A feature being important means the model relies on it to predict, not that it causes podium finishes.

  • Proxies: team Elo and points stand in for car speed, which the dataset does not measure. Raising a team's Elo would not make its car faster.
  • Shared credit: correlated position features split the credit arbitrarily.
  • Common cause: a good grid slot and a podium both come from the same fast car and driver.
  • Limited range: beyond the training range, the trees only repeat their last value.

Fairness.

  • No identity or nationality feature is used, and home_race was tested and dropped.
  • Age carries 0.3 % of the SHAP weight.
  • Rookies receive low probabilities because they have no history, not because of who they are.
  • The model inherits F1's dependence on established top teams, so it recognises a rising team only after its results arrive.

9. Known limitations

  • Qualifying-coverage drift. Before 1994 there is no qualifying data. The qualifying position is derived from the grid, assuming grid = qualifying order. That assumption is verified only for 1994–2002 (98.2 % exact). There are no lap times before 1994.
  • Era sensitivity.
    • The base rate (about 3 / field size), reliability (39–49 % of entries finished before 2000, 80–86 % in the 2010s–20s) and field size all changed strongly over time.
    • The test seasons are a new rules era (2022 ground-effect cars).
    • Circuit history does not carry over into that era (§7.1).
    • season and team_starts take values beyond the training range, which the trees cap.
    • The models lag mid-season swings in form (2024).
  • Small evaluation sets. Validation has 39 races, so many differences are within bootstrap noise, and the tuned validation scores are optimistic. The per-circuit (Q1) and per-team (Q3) rates have wide CIs.
  • Picking three per race. The models are not better than the grid at naming the three podium finishers; their value is calibrated confidence across races.
  • Missing information. There is no weather, tyre or practice-pace data, and retirements and safety cars cannot be foreseen.
  • Source inconsistencies kept as recorded. 100 races have repeated grid values (only 4 since 2000), there are 11 race-time / gap mismatches, and one row has more laps than the winner.
  • Definitions. Nationality is not the same as upbringing, and the status mapping records what failed, not why. The strict-definition check shows that Q3 is robust to this.

10. Conclusion

Careful cleaning and leakage control, verified by a time-travel test, produced a pre-race table on which every model clearly beats the obvious grid rule at ranking podium contenders. The selected random forest reaches a test PR-AUC of 0.714 and F1 of 0.686.

The gain comes from combining starting position with recent performance into well-calibrated probabilities, not from better picking of the three finishers. A simple logistic regression is statistically as good, which shows the signal is mostly additive.

Home advantage is absent once the grid is controlled for. Front-row conversion rose in the hybrid era, and mechanical reliability improved markedly after 2022.
